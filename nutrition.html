Nutrition basics

A plain-language guide to the numbers on every recipe card, and what they do and don’t tell you.

Calories

A calorie is a unit of energy. When food is described as containing 400 calories, that is a measure of how much energy your body can get from it. Everything you do uses energy, including the things you never think about: breathing, circulating blood, repairing tissue, keeping warm, thinking.

Energy needs differ enormously between people, and the reasons are mostly not mysterious. A taller person has more tissue to maintain. A more muscular person uses more at rest. Age changes things. So does how much you move, and that includes fidgeting, walking to the shop and standing up, not only formal exercise.

This is why a single number is never right for everyone, and why the calculator gives you a range rather than a target to hit exactly.

Protein

Protein provides the raw material your body uses to build and repair: muscle, skin, hair, enzymes, the antibodies in your immune system. Unlike fat, your body has no real store of it, so it needs a supply coming in regularly.

Protein is also the most filling of the three macronutrients, gram for gram. A meal with a decent amount of it tends to keep you going for longer than one without, which is why the recipes here usually build around a protein source even when the portion is small.

Common sources: meat, fish, eggs, dairy, beans, lentils, chickpeas, tofu, nuts and seeds.

Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are your body’s most readily available fuel. Your brain in particular runs largely on glucose, which is what most carbohydrate ends up as.

They come in forms that behave quite differently. Whole grains, beans, fruit and vegetables bring fibre and nutrients along with the energy, and tend to release it more slowly. Refined sources deliver energy faster with less alongside it. Neither is forbidden and neither is magic — it is a question of proportions across a week, not a rule about a single meal.

Common sources: bread, rice, pasta, potatoes, oats, couscous, fruit, beans and pulses.

Fat

Dietary fat is not optional. Vitamins A, D, E and K need fat to be absorbed at all, hormones are built from it, and every cell in your body has a membrane made partly of it. A diet with too little fat is genuinely uncomfortable as well as nutritionally poor.

Fat also carries flavour, which is more than a footnote. It is why a spoonful of pesto or a little feta can make a small plate of food feel like a proper meal. It is calorie-dense — more than twice as much energy per gram as protein or carbohydrate — so it moves the numbers quickly. That is a reason to measure it, not to avoid it.

Common sources: olive oil, nuts, seeds, avocado, oily fish, cheese, eggs.

Fibre

Fibre is the part of plant food your body doesn’t digest. That sounds useless and isn’t. It keeps your digestive system working properly, feeds the bacteria that live in your gut, and slows the release of energy from a meal, which helps it hold you for longer.

Most people eat considerably less of it than recommended. Chickpeas, beans, wholemeal bread, oats, vegetables with the skin left on and fruit all help, and they are also, conveniently, the things that make a small portion feel bigger.

Portion sizes

A calorie number tells you about energy. It tells you nothing at all about whether a meal will satisfy you, and those two things come apart constantly.

Three hundred calories of biscuits and three hundred calories of a chickpea bowl are the same figure and an entirely different experience. Volume matters. So does protein, fibre, temperature, texture, whether the food needs chewing, whether it looks like a proper meal on the plate, and how long it takes to eat.

This is the whole idea behind the recipes here. When a portion has to be modest, the useful lever is not eating less of something — it is choosing things that give you more food for the same number: vegetables with bulk, protein that lasts, texture that makes you slow down.

Balanced meals

There is no single correct plate, but a meal built from four components tends to work well and is easy to remember:

  • Protein — the part that keeps you full: chicken, fish, eggs, beans, dairy, tofu.
  • Carbohydrate — the fuel: rice, couscous, potato, bread, pasta.
  • Vegetables or fruit — volume, fibre, colour, and most of the micronutrients.
  • Fat — the flavour, and the thing that stops it feeling like a diet plate: olive oil, cheese, nuts, a spoonful of tahini or pesto.

Look at the Golden Hour Bowl and you can see all four. That is not a coincidence, and it is a more useful template than any specific recipe.

None of this needs to be perfect at every meal. Balance is something that happens across days, not within each plate.

Verified and estimated

Every recipe on this site is labelled one of two ways.

Verified means the figures come from the actual product packaging. Estimated means they are built from generic ingredient values, and the real figure will shift depending on the brands you buy.

Estimated numbers are shown with a tilde — ~360 kcal rather than 360 kcal — and rounded, deliberately. A figure like 361.47 kcal would look precise and would be false precision. Where a recipe’s numbers depend heavily on one product, the recipe page says so.
